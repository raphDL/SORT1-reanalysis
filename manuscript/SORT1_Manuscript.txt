ABSTRACT
Sequence-to-function models could accelerate the interpretation and engineering of non-coding regulatory DNA, but their performance across different applications remains incompletely characterized. Here, we benchmarked AlphaGenome at the well-established chromosome 1p13 SORT1 locus, evaluating its ability to prioritize causal variants, reconstruct regulatory mechanisms, approximate experimental mutagenesis screens, and nominate genome-engineering strategies. We compared AlphaGenome predictions with liver eQTL and caQTL data, HepG2 chromatin-contact maps, experimentally observed CRISPR repair products, and saturation-mutagenesis MPRA measurements. We further performed in silico mutagenesis, sequence scrambling, enhancer-transfer analyses, and cell-type-specific motif-replacement screens. AlphaGenome uniquely prioritized the LDL cholesterol-associated variant rs12740374 among 111 linked variants, recovered the creation of a C/EBP-binding site, and reproduced pre-existing contact preferences. Across 1,798 single-nucleotide substitutions surrounding rs12740374, predicted RNA, ATAC, and H3K27ac effects correlated strongly with MPRA measurements. However, performance varied between regulatory elements and was weaker for aggregate CRISPR outcomes, small deletions affecting distal SORT1 expression, and some locus-held-out predictions. AlphaGenome additionally nominated previously unrecognized sequence-sensitive regions, including a candidate FOX/forkhead-associated element, and defined a 315-bp enhancer interval as well as targeted motif-placing edits for potential engineering applications. Despite revealing limitations, the results establish AlphaGenome as a useful framework for prioritizing regulatory variants and designing experimental hypotheses.
The chromosome 1p13.3 SORT1 locus is a major common-variant determinant of LDL cholesterol and coronary artery disease risk. Its protective rs12740374-T allele creates a C/EBP-binding site and increases hepatic expression of SORT1, PSRC1 and CELSR2, yet the relationship between direct regulatory response and phenotype-relevant target-gene assignment remains unresolved. Here, we used AlphaGenome to reanalyze the locus across sequence, chromatin, and transcriptional modalities. AlphaGenome prioritized rs12740374 among linked variants and recovered its C/EBP-associated chromatin and transcriptional effects. The predicted direct RNA effect was largest for PSRC1, whereas observed HepG2 Hi-C and a held-out AlphaGenome contact model identified a strong pre-existing contact preference for distal SORT1, the gene implicated by mouse perturbation. Thus, sequence response, chromatin architecture, and organismal phenotype prioritized different aspects of the same multi-gene locus. AlphaGenome also predicted geometry-dependent effects of experimentally observed CRISPR repair products and reproduced the SORT1 saturation-mutagenesis landscape. Across 8,153 substitutions in six regulatory elements, agreement with saturation-mutagenesis measurements remained positive but heterogeneous, demonstrating generalization beyond SORT1 without implying uniform performance. Further in silico dissection nominated a 315-bp allele-dependent module with transferable promoter-proximal activity and distributed motif-family sensitivity. These results show that multimodal sequence models can reconstruct regulatory mechanisms while also exposing a central limitation of target-gene interpretation: the strongest direct sequence effect need not identify the gene most strongly connected by chromatin architecture or organismal phenotype.

INTRODUCTION
Deciphering the “code” embedded within the non-coding regulatory regions of the human genome has become a central challenge in modern genomics1–3. These regions contain the information that determines when and where genes are expressed by regulating processes such as transcription factor binding, chromatin accessibility, three-dimensional genome organization, and epigenetic state. Over the past two decades, genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have identified thousands of disease-associated single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs), the vast majority of which reside in non-coding DNA4,5. However, pinpointing the causal variants and uncovering the underlying molecular mechanisms has remained challenging, as functional validation traditionally relies on labor-intensive experimental screens6,7.
Beyond the characterization of individual SNPs, massively parallel reporter assays (MPRAs) have enabled the systematic identification of cis-regulatory elements, including enhancers, across non-coding regions8–11. In these assays, genomic sequences are tiled into overlapping fragments and individually tested for their ability to drive transcription, often in a cell type-specific manner. While highly informative, MPRAs require extensive experimental effort and are inherently limited in their ability to capture interactions between regulatory elements separated by larger genomic distances10,12. Finally, the therapeutic potential of understanding regulatory DNA has recently become apparent in the first FDA-approved CRISPR-based therapy, Casgevy, which restores fetal hemoglobin expression by disrupting an enhancer of BCL11A13. Yet identifying such therapeutically relevant targets still depends on extensive experiments, such as CRISPR screens14.
Sequence-to-function models harbor the potential to reduce labor-intensive experimental screening with accurate in silico prediction, particularly for non-coding genomic sequences3,15–17. Among the most advanced models is AlphaGenome, which combines a one megabase-scale sequence analysis window with single-nucleotide resolution and has the ability to predict the consequences of genetic variations across multiple modalities, including RNA expression, chromatin accessibility, transcription factor binding, and chromatin contacts across diverse cell types and tissues15. Such a model could have broad applications in three key areas: (1) It could prioritize causal disease-associated SNPs and reconstruct the regulatory mechanisms underlying specific loci. (2) It could function as an in silico alternative to MPRAs by identifying cis-regulatory elements within their native genomic context. (3) It could guide targeted genetic engineering strategies to precisely up- or downregulate gene expression in specific cell types, e.g., for therapeutic applications.
To evaluate whether AlphaGenome can deliver on these promises, we selected the well-characterized chromosome 1p13 locus, also known as SORT1 locus, as a benchmark. This locus regulates the expression of three genes, i.e., SORT1, CELSR2, and PSRC1. All of them are located within a one-megabase interval, which is well suited for an analysis by AlphaGenome. Importantly, the locus contains the extensively studied SNP rs12740374, creating a minor T allele that is strongly associated with reduced plasma low-density lipoprotein cholesterol levels and a lower risk of myocardial infarction18,19. Musunuru et al. demonstrated that this variant creates a binding site for CCAAT/enhancer-binding protein (C/EBP) transcription factors, resulting in increased expression of all three genes in hepatocytes19. Subsequent work by Wang et al. experimentally disrupted the rs12740374 C/EBP binding site and quantified the resulting transcriptional effects20. In parallel, Kircher et al. performed an MPRA across an approximately 600-bp region surrounding rs12740374 systematically measuring the effects of single-nucleotide substitutions and identifying regulatory regions extending beyond the C/EBP binding site21. Together, these complementary studies provide a comprehensive experimental framework against which AlphaGenome can be rigorously evaluated.
In this study, we reanalyzed these three complementary datasets centered on rs12740374 and the SORT1 locus using AlphaGenome. In doing so, we assess AlphaGenome’s ability to correctly identify rs12740374 as the causal regulatory variant and recapitulate cis-regulatory elements through an MPRA-like in silico screen. We then move beyond reproducing known findings to investigate whether AlphaGenome can nominate previously unrecognized regulatory features within the SORT1 locus, such as additional transcription factor binding sites. Finally, we leverage these predictions to identify targeted genetic engineering strategies that mimic the beneficial effects of rs12740374, thereby exploring the potential of sequence-to-function models to guide therapeutic genome engineering.


RESULTS
AlphaGenome prioritizes rs12740374 and recovers its regulatory mechanism and pre-existing contact preference for SORT1
Musunuru et al. identified rs12740374 in the 3′ untranslated region (3’UTR) of CELSR2, within a 6.1-kb regulatory interval between CELSR2 and PSRC1 and demonstrated that, by creating a C/EBP-binding site, the SNP increases hepatic expression of all three genes within the locus, i.e., SORT1, PSRC1 and CELSR2 (Fig. 1A)19. We asked whether AlphaGenome could correctly predict these molecular effects caused by rs12740374. To generate such AlphaGenome predictions, we used a 524-kb sequence centered on rs12740374 (Fig. 1B, 70-kb sequence window displayed) in the context of the human liver cell line HepG2 (ChIP-seq), mean liver tissue (DNase and ATAC-seq) and strand-resolved liver signal (RNA-seq). As previously demonstrated15, the substitution of the reference G allele with rs12740374, i.e., the minor T allele, resulted in a sharp increase in predicted CEBPA ChIP-seq signal at the variant position indicating the creation of a C/EBP binding site. Moreover, the T allele increased local liver ATAC and RNA signal. The corresponding change in DNase signal was comparatively modest. Together, these predictions accurately recapitulate the experimentally established molecular mechanism by which rs12740374 creates a C/EBP-binding site and increases local regulatory activity.
We next tested whether AlphaGenome could prioritize rs12740374 and decorrelate it from other SNPs that are present on the same allele. For this analysis, we included 111 GWAS-identified SNPs spanning approximately 127 kb that were significantly associated with low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C). Using these SNPs, we detected an association with the expression of SORT1, PSRC1 and CELSR2 in liver tissue for many of the variants, by analyzing GTEx v7 liver eQTL effects (Fig. 1C, left)22. This is consistent with a strong linkage disequilibrium in this region and demonstrates a major challenge of GWAS, i.e., differentiating causal SNPs from variants linked by co-inheritance. For the prioritization analysis, we re-scored all 111 variants using 1,048,576-bp inputs, the AlphaGenome ALL_FOLDS model, and the exon-masked RNA variant scorer, which calculates the predicted log-fold change in RNA-seq signal (Fig. 1C, middle). rs12740374 was the only variant that had a predicted effect within each of the three genes with the largest effect observed for PSRC1 (0.6047), followed by CELSR2 (0.4188) and SORT1 (0.0488), corresponding to predicted increases of approximately 83.1%, 52.0%, and 5.0%, respectively. The effects of the other 110 variants were substantially smaller within each gene. Therefore, when comparing the experimental eQTL data with the predicted AlphaGenome scores, only a very small correlation is observed (Pearson r=0.25, 0.18, and 0.21 for SORT1, PSRC1 and CELSR2, respectively). 
This divergence likely reflects the different quantities being measured. AlphaGenome predicts the direct molecular consequence of each sequence variant, whereas population eQTL signals are distributed across variants in linkage disequilibrium (LD). To test whether rs12740374 alone could explain the observed eQTL pattern, we weighted its predicted effect according to allele co-inheritance in phased European 1000 Genomes haplotypes (Fig. 1C, right; Fig. S1)23. The resulting LD-tagging model closely reproduced the experimental eQTL profiles (Pearson r = 0.87, 0.91 and 0.91 for SORT1, PSRC1 and CELSR2, respectively). This analysis demonstrates that the predicted effect of rs12740374 is sufficient to account for the broader population association pattern. The results indicate that AlphaGenome successfully distinguishes the direct regulatory variant from neighboring SNPs linked through linkage disequilibrium. 
Nevertheless, the direct predicted RNA effect was substantially larger for PSRC1 than for SORT1, despite SORT1 showing the strongest liver eQTL association and being the gene experimentally implicated in cholesterol regulation. We therefore asked whether AlphaGenome could reliably detect chromatin architecture differences between the promoters of the three genes. For this, we examined experimental in situ Hi-C data obtained from HepG224. We were able to identify a previously described contact25 between the rs12740374 region and the SORT1 promoter that was elevated by a 7.77-fold compared to what would be expected according to the genomic-distance and that ranked at the 99.68th percentile of contacts at the same distance (Fig. 1D; Fig. S1A). By comparison, CELSR2 and PSRC1 contacts were 1.43- and 1.24-fold above expectation and ranked at the 45.11th and 21.06th percentiles, respectively. 
We next asked whether AlphaGenome could reproduce this promoter-contact hierarchy. However, since AlphaGenome may have been trained on data from this locus, there is a risk that any agreement with the experimental Hi-C map merely reflects training-data reuse. We therefore used FOLD_0, a model trained while withholding a subset of chromosome 1 intervals that included the SORT1 locus (Fig. 1E). As observed and predicted maps undergo different preprocessing, their absolute values are not directly comparable. We therefore compared them using percentile ranks among contacts at the same genomic distance. The held-out AlphaGenome FOLD_0 prediction reproduced the promoter ranking, placing SORT1 at the 99.67th percentile, compared with the 85.84th percentile for CELSR2 and the 32.57th percentile for PSRC1 (Fig. 1F). This finding held true when using only the TSS-containing bin instead of a 3x3-bin analysis (Fig. S1B). AlphaGenome therefore recovered the preferential baseline contact between the rs12740374 region and the distal SORT1 promoter even when the locus was excluded from training.
We then tested whether the rs12740374 T minor allele was predicted to alter this baseline chromatin contact present in the major G allele. The rs12740374 T allele produced only minor predicted changes in promoter contact (+1.11% for CELSR2, +0.18% for PSRC1 and +1.57% for SORT1; Fig. S1C), suggesting that the variant acts within a pre-existing chromatin architecture rather than establishing a new promoter interaction. Consistent with previous reports26, AlphaGenome predicted the SORT1 promoter to be the preferred interaction partner across all 12 contact-map contexts, where it ranked above the 99th percentile in every case (Fig. S1D).
As the FOLD_0 analysis predicted the Hi-C contacts well, we wanted to ensure that this model could also reliably predict the differences in RNA expression (Fig. S2A, B) and correctly prioritize rs12740374. Reassuringly, rs12740374 remained the variant with the largest absolute predicted RNA effect for SORT1, CELSR2, and PSRC1 (Fig. S2C). Its predicted magnitude was reduced under FOLD_0 for SORT1 and PSRC1 but was nearly unchanged for CELSR2. Rank concordance across all 111 variants was modest (Spearman ρ = 0.42, 0.27, and 0.02, respectively), reflecting instability among the substantially smaller effects assigned to neighboring variants. In conclusion, these analyses indicate that rs12740374 remains the highest-priority regulatory variant even when predictions are generated using a model that excluded this locus during training.
Finally, we wanted to evaluate whether AlphaGenome could prioritize rs12740374 when given reported chromatin-accessibility data, i.e., liver caQTL across 80 local variants27 (Fig. S3A). As observed for the eQTL analysis, direct ATAC variant scores nominated rs12740374 as the causal variant, but showed little correlation with regional caQTL effects, whereas incorporating linkage disequilibrium restored close agreement with the experimental data under both training regimes (Fig. S3C-D, F-G). These findings again indicate that AlphaGenome assigns the direct sequence effect to rs12740374, while population-level associations are propagated to neighboring variants through linkage disequilibrium. 
Together, these analyses show that AlphaGenome accurately identifies rs12740374 as the causal regulatory variant, recovers its experimentally established molecular mechanism, and faithfully reproduces the native chromatin architecture that links the regulatory element to the SORT1 promoter. These findings establish AlphaGenome as a reliable framework for interrogating regulatory variation at the SORT1 locus.

AlphaGenome predicts the regulatory consequences of CRISPR repair products and recapitulates an MPRA analysis of the rs12740374 locus enhancer
Having established that AlphaGenome correctly identifies rs12740374 as the causal variant, we next asked whether it could predict the regulatory consequences of CRISPR-induced sequence changes. For this, we benchmarked AlphaGenome against the study by Wang et al., in which Cas9 disruption of the rs12740374 enhancer reduced PSRC1 and SORT1 expression in primary human hepatocytes (Fig. 2A)20. We identified the 50 most frequent unique indel sequences observed in primary human hepatocytes or locus-humanized mouse liver and scored each sequence using AlphaGenome. AlphaGenome predicted reduced RNA expression for 26 of 50 edited alleles for SORT1, 38 of 50 for PSRC1, and 38 of 50 for CELSR2, with median changes of −1.22%, −36.53%, and −19.82%, respectively (Fig. 2B). However, when frequency-weighting these predictions according to how often the corresponding indels were observed experimentally, we observed little overall change in predicted expression despite the experimentally observed transcriptional reduction (Fig. S4A, B). To understand this discrepancy, we systematically modeled deletions spanning the Cas9 cut site. Rather than deletion length, AlphaGenome predictions depended primarily on repair geometry (Fig. 2C). Deletions extending at least two nucleotides downstream disrupted the C/EBP motif and consistently reduced predicted PSRC1 and CELSR2 expression, whereas many upstream-skewed deletions preserved or recreated the motif and therefore produced neutral or increased expression (Fig. S4C–D). 
Although AlphaGenome successfully captured the molecular consequences of individual repair products, it did not reproduce the experimentally observed aggregate transcriptional response. We therefore next asked whether AlphaGenome could more generally recapitulate experimentally measured regulatory effects obtained from large-scale screens, such as MPRAs. To benchmark AlphaGenome across a substantially larger mutational landscape, we next analyzed the saturation-mutagenesis MPRA generated by Kircher et al.21, comprising 1,800 substitutions spanning the rs12740374 enhancer (Fig. 2D). Across 1,798 matched substitutions, experimental reporter effects correlated strongly with predicted HepG2 RNA (Pearson r = 0.84; Spearman r = 0.67), ATAC (r = 0.80; r = 0.64), and H3K27ac (r = 0.83; r = 0.66) effects (Fig. 2E).
We next asked whether this strong agreement was specific to the SORT1 enhancer or generalized to independent regulatory elements. To test this, we applied a cell-line-matched accessibility benchmark to six additional regulatory elements evaluated in Kircher et al. (Fig. 2F). The harmonized DNase comparison included 8,153 substitutions and produced positive but strongly heterogeneous element-level Spearman correlations, ranging from r = 0.05 for FOXE1 to r = 0.69 for SORT1. The random-effects average was r = 0.42 (95% CI 0.17–0.62), but heterogeneity was extensive (Q = 810.2, df = 5, P < 10−16; I2 = 99.4%), making the individual element estimates more informative than the pooled value. Matched ATAC predictions were additionally available for F9, LDLR, PKLR, and SORT1. In this same four-element subset, the DNase and ATAC random-effects estimates were nearly identical (r = 0.55, 95% CI 0.38–0.68, and r = 0.53, 95% CI 0.40–0.64, respectively). Thus, the weak FOXE1 and MYC results were not caused by systematically weaker DNase scoring. Substantial heterogeneity was also present when only evaluating one cell type. Among the three HepG2 elements, DNase correlations ranged from r = 0.34 for LDLR to r = 0.69 for SORT1. This data suggests that AlphaGenome’s performance remains element and context dependent.
To test whether AlphaGenome’s performance depended on locus-specific information encountered during training, we repeated the SORT1 MPRA benchmark using FOLD_0 (Fig. S5). Predictive performance decreased but remained substantial for the three-gene RNA mean, ATAC, and H3K27ac signals. Pearson correlations declined from 0.84 to 0.75 for RNA, from 0.80 to 0.68 for ATAC, and from 0.83 to 0.73 for H3K27ac. RNA correlations also remained high for PSRC1 and CELSR2, decreasing from 0.83 to 0.73 and from 0.85 to 0.74, respectively. In contrast, the SORT1-specific RNA correlation fell from 0.79 to −0.16. Thus, AlphaGenome generalized to the held-out locus for local regulatory readouts and nearby genes, whereas accurate prediction of distal SORT1 expression depended strongly on locus exposure during training. 
We next asked whether differences in predictive performance across regulatory elements could similarly be explained by training exposure (Fig. S6). Each non-SORT1 element was rescored using the model that withheld its genomic fold and compared with the corresponding ALL_FOLDS result. DNase correlations changed only modestly for most elements. Spearman ρ decreased from 0.47 to 0.44 for F9, from 0.05 to 0.04 for FOXE1, from 0.17 to 0.12 for MYC, and from 0.63 to 0.56 for PKLR. LDLR was the only element to improve, from 0.34 to 0.49. Comparable changes were observed for matched ATAC predictions. Therefore, locus exposure did not explain the marked element-to-element variation in AlphaGenome performance, which instead appears to reflect differences in regulatory context. 
Because the CRISPR repair analysis suggested that AlphaGenome underestimates deletion effects on SORT1 expression, we specifically examined the 126 single-base deletions included in the MPRA (Fig. S5D). As before, the three-gene, PSRC1 and CELSR2 predictions correlated reasonably well (r = 0.81, 0.78 and 0.82 for ALL_FOLDS, 0.68, 0.65 and 0.70 for FOLD_0). Interestingly, despite correlating well (r = 0.74) in the ALL_FOLDS model, SORT1 effect sizes remained very small and completely deteriorated in FOLD_0 (r= -0.23). This finding is in concordance with the CRISPR repair analysis predicting only minor expression changes in SORT1 expression and might suggest that AlphaGenome’s prediction accuracy decreases for small deletion effects in distant locations in this locus.
Together, these analyses demonstrate that AlphaGenome accurately predicts the regulatory consequences of sequence variants across both CRISPR repair products and saturation mutagenesis assays. Performance was strongest for single-nucleotide substitutions and remained dependent on regulatory element and genomic context, with distal SORT1 expression and small deletions proving particularly challenging. These results indicate that AlphaGenome can approximate MPRA-scale functional screens in silico while highlighting current limitations for predicting complex enhancer–promoter interactions.

AlphaGenome nominates novel regulatory sites and a 315-bp rs12740374 enhancer interval
Having benchmarked AlphaGenome against experimental datasets, we next asked whether it could identify previously unrecognized regulatory features at the rs12740374 locus. We first screened every possible single-nucleotide substitution within a 100-kb interval centered on rs12740374 and scored coordinated activation of SORT1, PSRC1 and CELSR2 (Fig. 3A). rs12740374 G>T remained the strongest coordinated activator in the entire interval, while only one weaker secondary cluster was detected. These findings suggest that the coordinated activation of all three genes is a distinctive property of rs12740374 and motivated a higher-resolution analysis of its surrounding enhancer. 
We therefore performed saturation mutagenesis across a 501-bp region surrounding rs12740374 while maintaining the activating T allele (Fig. 3B, S7A). Each position was substituted with the three alternative nucleotides, generating 1,503 edited sequences. As expected, the largest predicted decreases clustered within the established C/EBP-binding motif. To identify additional regulatory features, we ranked overlapping 12-bp windows according to their predicted reduction in mean RNA expression across SORT1, PSRC1 and CELSR2. Besides the C/EBP motif, AlphaGenome identified six non-overlapping sequence-sensitive regions (H1-H6), five upstream and one downstream of rs12740374. The strongest, H5, produced a reduction comparable to reverting rs12740374 itself, suggesting that enhancer activity depends on additional regulatory information beyond the C/EBP site alone. 
Next, we performed a motif analysis, which recovered the expected C/EBP/bZIP family at the positive-control interval and nominated additional motif families within H1-H6, including FOX/forkhead motifs in H2 and H5 (Fig. 3C)28. Because motif enrichment alone does not establish occupancy, these assignments should be regarded as mechanistic hypotheses. Repeating the analysis with the held-out FOLD_0 model yielded highly similar window rankings (Spearman ρ = 0.96), indicating that identification of these candidate regulatory regions did not depend on locus exposure during training (Fig. S7B,C). 
Having identified candidate regulatory elements, we next asked whether AlphaGenome could define a minimal enhancer suitable for targeted engineering. For this, we performed scrambling and progressive sequence-restoration experiments (Fig. 3D). This analysis showed that the C/EBP motif alone retained only 3.5% of enhancer activity and that restoring upstream sequence recovered substantially more activity than restoring downstream sequence, indicating that regulatory information is concentrated upstream of rs12740374 (Fig. E, Fig. S8A). However, neither arm alone reached the predefined 70% activity threshold, suggesting that both contribute to full enhancer function. 
A two-dimensional boundary scan identified a 315-bp interval extending 179 bp upstream and 135 bp downstream of rs12740374 as the shortest sequence retaining robust enhancer activity of 70.9% of the intact RNA expression across scramble replicates (Fig. 3F; Fig. S8B, C). Although nearby windows of similar size often performed substantially worse (Fig. S8D), this interval consistently preserved approximately 70% of intact enhancer activity, indicating that sequence composition rather than length determines functional sufficiency. Finally, we examined the contribution of individual components within the 315-bp enhancer (Fig. 3G, S8E). Besides the expected requirement for the C/EBP motif, H5 emerged as the strongest non-C/EBP regulatory element, whereas H1-H4 and H6 contributed comparatively little. H5 contains a predicted FOX/forkhead motif, consistent with previously reported FOXA1/FOXA2 occupancy at this locus29,30, but its functional contribution has not been experimentally demonstrated. Together, these analyses show that AlphaGenome can move beyond reproducing known biology to nominate previously unrecognized regulatory components and define compact enhancer architectures suitable for engineering.

AlphaGenome predicts transferable activity of rs12740374 enhancer and engineerable tissue-specific sequence responses
Having identified a compact 315-bp enhancer, we next asked whether AlphaGenome could use it to guide genetic engineering. Specifically, we tested whether the enhancer would be predicted to retain activity when transferred to new genomic loci. For this, we placed the rs12740374-T enhancer upstream of recipient transcription start sites (TSSs) using rs12740374-G and composition-matched scrambled sequences as a control. Since we found that AlphaGenome’s predictions were more accurate for substitutions than insertions, we inserted these sequences in a strand-aware, length-preserving replacement of the native sequence rather than true insertions (Fig. 4A). Recipient genes were selected from the 500 lowest-, middle- and highest-expressing genes in the Human Protein Atlas (HPA) liver dataset31. Native AlphaGenome liver RNA predictions correlated strongly with HPA expression across 20,006 genes (Pearson r = 0.82; Spearman ρ = 0.81), supporting the use of these expression-defined cohorts (Fig. S9A).
We first examined whether enhancer activity depended on genomic placement by replacing native promoter-proximal sequences at varying distances from the 100 lowly expressed genes. As expected32,33, predicted activation was strongest close to the TSS and progressively declined with distance (Fig. 4B, S9B). We therefore used the 30-bp position for all subsequent transfer experiments. Using this optimized placement, we next tested the relative activation of 1,471 recipient genes consisting of the lowest-, middle- and highest-expression cohort (Fig. 4C). The rs12740374-T enhancer increased the predicted expression in nearly all low- (99.2%, median fold change 7.12) and medium-expression genes (96.7%, median fold change 1.63) and in most highly expressed genes (81.9%, median fold change 0.67). Activation was greatest in low-expression recipients, whereas highly expressed genes showed more modest gains, reflecting their higher baseline expression (Fig. S9C–E). The scrambled replacement frequently reduced expression, especially in highly expressing genes, indicating that replacement of promoter-proximal native sequence can itself be disruptive. Overall, AlphaGenome predicts that the rs12740374-T enhancer interval can be used to engineer gene activation in the liver.
We next asked whether AlphaGenome could predict enhancer activity beyond promoter-proximal insertion sites. One of AlphaGenome’s key advantages is its ability to process input sequences of up to 1 megabase, making it theoretically capable of predicting effects at regions distal to an engineered site. Our previous analyses indicated that AlphaGenome could identify a distal Hi-C contact site with the SORT1 TSS and predict associated changes in gene expression. We therefore investigated whether it could also predict effects at other experimentally validated distal Hi-C contacts. To this end, we identified 1,860 validated distal (>50 kb) high contacts involving promoter sequences across chromosome 1 and inserted the 315-bp T- or G-containing enhancers at these contact sites (Fig. 4D). For each promoter, two sites at a same-range distance, but with low contact frequency served as controls. The response was quantified as the predicted effect at the high-contact site minus the mean predicted effect at the two low-contact sites.
AlphaGenome predicted stronger responses at high-contact sites for 70.9% of promoters located 50–150 kb away. However, this fraction decreased with distance, falling to 60.1% at 150–300 kb and 55.6% at 300–500 kb (Fig. 4E). Across the 50–500 kb range, the fraction of positive interactions generally increased with the experimentally measured difference in contact frequency between the high- and low-contact sites (Fig. 4F). Nevertheless, effect sizes remained small, and individual predictions were highly variable (Fig. S10A). Overall, predicted contact-dependent activation weakened with increasing genomic distance and was no longer distinguishable beyond 500 kb. This decline may reflect biological distance constraints, restrictions due to the 1 megabase input window or limitations of long-range prediction within AlphaGenome. 
Beyond transferring an existing enhancer, we next asked whether AlphaGenome could design new sequence edits that activate a target locus in a cell type-specific manner using the SORT1 locus as an exemplary target site. We first confirmed that AlphaGenome predicted the strongest positive rs12740374-T allele-related RNA effects in the liver for all three genes (Fig. 4G). Interestingly, this pattern was substantially preserved by FOLD_0 (Fig. S10B), indicating that this prediction was not caused by training data exposure. Next, we chose two non-liver cell types as engineering targets, i.e., CD8+ memory T cells and CD14+ monocytes. For these, we performed a 100-kb single-nucleotide mutagenesis scan across the SORT1 locus and examined scored coordinated RNA upregulation of SORT1, PSRC1 and CELSR2. For this analysis, the sites resulting in high coordinated upregulation scores differed among liver and the immune cells (Fig. 4H). Because no single substitution matched the effect of rs12740374, we tested whether replacing native sequence stretches with target cell-type-associated transcription-factor motifs of identical length would result in higher predicted scores (Fig. 4I). The resulting maps nominated distinct context-dependent responsive regions and motif families, including stronger HNF1B/HNF4A-associated responses in liver and SPI1, RUNX1 and IRF8-associated responses in CD8+ memory T cells and CD14+ monocytes (Fig. 4J), which can be validated in downstream engineering experiments. Together, these analyses demonstrate that AlphaGenome can support two complementary genome engineering tasks, i.e., transferring functional enhancer sequences between loci and designing cell type-specific regulatory edits within endogenous loci. These findings illustrate the potential of sequence-to-function models to guide therapeutic genome engineering.

DISCUSSION
In this study, we evaluated the potential of AlphaGenome for several applications in the field of genetics and bioengineering, such as nominating SNPs, predicting effects of cis-regulatory elements in non-coding regions or suggesting edits for targeted genetic engineering. To benchmark AlphaGenome and answer these questions, we used the extensively characterized SORT1 1p13 locus. Overall, the model showed strong but non-uniform performance. It prioritized rs12740374 over 110 other phenotype-associated variants, recovered the creation of a C/EBP-binding site and the associated changes in chromatin and transcriptional outputs, and reproduced the pre-existing contact preference of the locus for the distal SORT1 promoter. It also captured the sequence-geometry dependence of individual CRISPR repair products and closely recapitulated the saturation-mutagenesis landscape surrounding rs12740374. However, performance varied substantially between regulatory elements and was weaker for aggregate CRISPR outcomes, small deletions affecting a distal gene, and locus-held-out prediction of SORT1 expression. The results therefore support AlphaGenome as a powerful framework for interpreting regulatory sequence while illustrating that causal-SNP prioritization, mechanistic reconstruction, and prediction of CRISPR- or MPRA-derived mutational effects are distinct tasks and may lead to varying performance levels.
AlphaGenome's ability to nominate causal SNPs has also been examined independently by Sweeney and Kang, who evaluated AlphaGenome and other models across fine-mapped eQTL datasets34. Rather than using a locus with an experimentally established regulatory driver, they analyzed cross-cohort replication as a proxy for causality across many loci. In this study, AlphaGenome generally provided one of the strongest prioritizations among the tested models. Moreover, they developed a downstream framework that integrates AlphaGenome predictions with statistical posterior inclusion probabilities. This combined approach improved the confident prioritization of variants, illustrating how AlphaGenome predictions can complement association-based evidence.
The saturation-mutagenesis analyses in this study further suggest that AlphaGenome can serve as an in silico analogue of an MPRA for some applications. At the rs12740374 enhancer, predicted HepG2 RNA, ATAC and H3K27ac effects agreed strongly with the experimental reporter landscape, indicating that the model can identify regulatory bases and rank thousands of substitutions without synthesizing and assaying each construct. Because predictions are generated in a megabase-scale genomic context, such screens can also retain native sequence surroundings and, in principle, evaluate effects on endogenous genes and distal interactions that are not represented in short episomal reporter constructs8,21. The broader six-element benchmark, however, argues against treating AlphaGenome as a general replacement for MPRAs. Correlations were positive but highly heterogeneous, including weak performance at some elements and substantial variation even among HepG2 assays. A more realistic near-term role for AlphaGenome is therefore to pre-screen regulatory sequences and prioritize the most informative variants and elements for experimental validation. 
Another central aim of this study was to ask whether AlphaGenome can find regulatory features that were not supplied as established ground truth and nominate edits for targeted genetic-engineering engineering. The native SORT1 locus-scan nominated six sequence-sensitive windows outside the known C/EBP site, with H5 emerging as the strongest additional component and showing compatibility with FOX/forkhead motifs. While reported FOXA1/FOXA2 occupancy at the locus makes the H5 hypothesis biologically plausible35,36, it is important to note that the analyzes performed in this paper do not establish transcription-factor binding, or regulatory necessity. 
The same distinction applies to the genetic-engineering analyses. AlphaGenome predicted that the rs12740374-T enhancer module could increase liver gene expression in many recipient genes when placed close to their promoters or into high-contact sites, and targeted edits integrating liver-, T-cell- or monocyte-associated motifs could be used to increase expression at the endogenous locus in the respective cell types. These results illustrate a potentially important design workflow in which a sequence-to-function model is used to select regulatory modules, positions and motif replacements before genome editing, analogous to using predictive models to narrow CRISPR screens or optimize therapeutic regulatory sequences13,14,37. However, it is important to note that none of these design predictions were benchmarked against prospective engineering data and require direct testing.
An important limitation is that AlphaGenome’s concordance in direction or overall rankings is not equivalent to an accurate prediction of absolute effect size. AlphaGenome predictions correlated with several experimental measurements, including the Kircher MPRA results and native HPA-derived liver expression across genes, but the experimental and predicted outputs frequently differed in their definitions, units and dynamic ranges (Figs. S5 and S9A). These comparisons therefore establish relative concordance rather than an accurate quantitative calibration. Within this study, AlphaGenome was consequently more informative for nominating and comparing candidate SNPs, regulatory sequences or targeted edits than for predicting their exact experimental effect sizes.
In relation to other sequence-to-function models, AlphaGenome's main practical advantage is the combination of megabase-scale context, fine output resolution, multiple regulatory modalities and contact-map prediction in one model3,15. Enformer and Borzoi established the value of long-range sequence models for expression and chromatin prediction, whereas models such as Sei and specialized tools for accessibility, splicing or chromatin architecture offer alternative trade-offs in task specialization, resolution and computational cost16,17,34. The AlphaGenome paper reported strong performance relative to both generalist and specialized baselines. Nevertheless, our study did not directly compare AlphaGenome with alternative models at the SORT1 locus. We therefore cannot infer that the observed strengths or failures are unique to AlphaGenome, nor that AlphaGenome would be the best model for every analysis performed here. 
Several additional limitations affect interpretation of the present results. HepG2 is a hepatic cancer cell line rather than primary liver, and the analyses combine HepG2 measurements with liver-tissue prediction tracks. Cell-state differences may therefore contribute to both successes and discrepancies. Moreover, AlphaGenome predicts molecular consequences within its training scope, not the downstream contribution of gene function, development, environment or physiology to LDL cholesterol and coronary disease15. We observed a weak held-out prediction of distal SORT1 expression, small predicted deletion effects, and a declining contact-associated transfer signal in distal regions. Because only one locus was examined in depth, we cannot determine whether these observations reflect a general difficulty with long-range enhancer-gene effects, a limitation of the 1-Mb input window, assay-specific noise, or features peculiar to this locus. Similarly, the model's strong local substitution performance and weaker behavior for aggregate repair outcomes may represent a general perturbation-class limitation or a locus-specific interaction between repair products and distal regulation. Finally, the study focuses on a single, unusually well-characterized locus and does not constitute genome-wide validation of every AlphaGenome modality. The results should therefore be interpreted as a detailed case study that identifies both promising applications and concrete failure modes, rather than as a universal performance estimate.
Overall, AlphaGenome was most convincing when used to prioritize a strong single-nucleotide regulatory variant, reconstruct its local molecular mechanism and triage dense substitution landscapes. AlphaGenome can plausibly reduce experimental search spaces, nominate new cis-regulatory components and guide the design of genome-engineering experiments. Prospective testing of the H5/FOX hypothesis, the 315-bp module, distal transfer behavior and cell-type-specific motif edits will be essential to determine whether these in silico nominations translate into robust regulatory control. When used as hypothesis generators and prioritization layers rather than as a substitute for causal experiments, multimodal sequence-to-function models could substantially accelerate the interpretation and engineering of non-coding regulatory loci.

METHODS
Genomic coordinates, annotations, and reference sequences
Manuscript coordinates are reported using 1-based genomic positions. Python sequence operations used 0-based, half-open intervals. All genomic coordinates are reported on the human GRCh38 assembly. rs12740374 was represented as chr1:109,274,968 G>T, with G designated the reference and LDL-raising allele and T the minor, LDL-lowering allele. Reference sequences were obtained from the hg38 reference genome using a local indexed FASTA or, where indicated, the UCSC sequence endpoint. Gene coordinates, strands, and transcription start sites (TSSs) were obtained from GENCODE release 4638. Unless stated otherwise, analyses focused on SORT1, PSRC1, and CELSR2. Coordinates from source publications reported on earlier assemblies were lifted to GRCh38 and verified against the local reference as described in the corresponding subsections. The exact AlphaGenome RNA-seq track names, zero-based RNA-output metadata indices, assay types, and strand assignments used for each gene are provided in Supplementary Table 1. 
AlphaGenome predictions and output conventions
Predictions were generated with the AlphaGenome Python client (version 0.5.1) through the AlphaGenome API15. Unless a held-out analysis is explicitly specified, analyses used the default ALL_FOLDS model served by the API at the time of each run. For every prediction run, the requested training regime, API-client version, run date, genomic interval, input-sequence hash, output metadata and output-file checksum were recorded in the deposited run manifests. Held-out analyses explicitly requested the FOLD_0 model, for which data fold 0, i.e., the fold containing the rs12740374 interval, was excluded from training.
Input length was analysis-specific: 524,288 bp for locus-scale scans, perturbation designs, and the liver caQTL analysis; 1,048,576 bp for the 111-variant RNA rescoring and transfer analyses; and 16,384 or 131,072 bp for the Kircher accessibility and RNA benchmarks, respectively.
Two forms of AlphaGenome output were used, and they were not treated as interchangeable:
1. Sequence-prediction analyses measured the change in predicted RNA signal within a defined TSS window. RNA output was summarized as the mean predicted signal in a ±2-kb window around the relevant GENCODE TSS, and the effect of a perturbation was the perturbed prediction minus the corresponding unmodified or otherwise specified baseline prediction.
2. Variant-scorer analyses used the recommended AlphaGenome variant scorers and took the scorer's gene-by-track effect values directly. These raw scores are reported in native AlphaGenome units.
Percentage changes were calculated only where explicitly stated, and only from sequence-predicted RNA values. Because these scoring schemes answer different questions and are differently normalized, their absolute magnitudes are not numerically comparable across analyses, and no cross-analysis comparison of raw magnitudes is made in the manuscript. Liver predictions used ontology term UBERON:0002107 and HepG2 predictions used EFO:0001187 unless otherwise specified.
Locus-scale prediction of the rs12740374 allele effect
For visualization of the molecular effects of rs12740374 (Figure 1B), reference- and alternate-allele predictions were generated in a 524,288-bp interval centered on the variant. Requested outputs included RNA-seq, DNase, ATAC, and transcription-factor ChIP-seq. The displayed interval was chr1:109,250,000–109,320,000. Tracks were selected by assay, ontology, transcription factor, and strand metadata. Matching liver DNase and ATAC tracks were averaged, RNA tracks were separated by strand, and the HepG2 CEBPA ChIP-seq track was used to display the predicted signal gain at the variant position.
LDL-C variant selection, liver eQTL comparison, and tagging model
LDL-C association statistics were obtained from the 2013 Global Lipids Genetics Consortium dataset39 and lifted to GRCh38. Variants with P < 5 × 10⁻⁸ within the approximately 131-kb prediction interval were retained, yielding 111 variants. For each variant, the LDL-raising and LDL-lowering alleles were assigned from the sign of the reported GWAS effect. GTEx v7 liver all-pairs eQTL data for SORT1, CELSR2, and PSRC1 were matched by genomic position and oriented so that positive values represented the effect of the LDL-lowering allele22. Complete eQTL data for all three genes were available for 108 of the 111 variants; correlations involving observed eQTL effects were calculated on this subset.
All 111 variants were scored with the ALL_FOLDS model using 1,048,576-bp inputs and the recommended AlphaGenome exon-masked RNA variant scorer (GeneMaskLFCScorer). Outputs were restricted to the liver ontology (UBERON:0002107). For each gene, three model RNA-seq tracks were retained: strand-specific poly(A) RNA-seq, strand-specific total RNA-seq, and unstranded poly(A) RNA-seq. For strand-specific tracks, minus-strand tracks were used for SORT1 and PSRC1; plus-strand tracks were used for CELSR2. The same three strand categories were used for every variant, yielding three track-level scores for each variant–gene combination. Track-level natural-log fold changes were oriented as LDL-lowering minus LDL-raising and averaged within each gene. rs12740374 was ranked against the other 110 variants by absolute mean predicted effect within each gene. 
To compare the FOLD_0 with the ALL_FOLDS model predictions, we used the frozen set of 111 LDL-C-associated variants and rescored them using the FOLD_0 model. Each variant was evaluated independently and no LD-tagging contribution was included in the analysis. The input interval, reference and alternate alleles, 1,048,576-bp sequence length, exon-masked RNA variant scorer, liver RNA tracks, strand-compatibility filtering, track averaging, and orientation to the LDL-lowering allele were identical to the ALL_FOLDS analysis. Agreement between training regimes was evaluated separately for each gene using Spearman correlation across the 111 direct scores and the absolute rank of rs12740374.
To test whether the broad regional eQTL pattern could be explained by variants tagging rs12740374, allele-oriented tagging coefficients were estimated from the phased high-coverage GRCh38 1000 Genomes call set for 3,202 samples23, restricted to the 503 unrelated European individuals (CEU, FIN, GBR, IBS, and TSI) defined in the Phase 3 sample panel40. For each variant, let X indicate a haplotype carrying that variant's LDL-lowering allele and let T indicate a haplotype carrying rs12740374-T. The tagging coefficient was defined as
β_tag = P(T = 1 | X = 1) − P(T = 1 | X = 0)
and was set to missing when P(X) was 0 or 1. The propagated prediction for a variant was its direct exon-masked score plus β_tag multiplied by the exon-masked score for rs12740374. For rs12740374 itself the propagated value was set to its direct effect, so that its own score was not counted twice. This construction assumes rs12740374 to be the driver and was therefore used as an explanatory tagging model. Pearson correlations between observed liver eQTL effects and either direct or tagging-model predictions were calculated on variants with finite values for both quantities.
Observed contact-map analysis
Observed chromatin contacts were obtained from the public merged-replicate HepG2 in situ Hi-C dataset distributed by the 4D Nucleome Data Portal24 as 4DNFICSTCJQZ.hic (GRCh38; DpnII). The companion cooler record, 4DNFIS6HAUPP.mcool, is flagged by the portal for duplicate pixels produced by an older cooler version. We therefore used the .hic file. KR-balanced observed/expected matrices were streamed with hic-straw at 2-kb resolution.


The primary promoter-contact statistic was the mean of a 3 × 3-bin window centered on the rs12740374 bin and the promoter bin in the 2-kb matrix, corresponding to an approximately 6-kb window on each axis. For each promoter, a distance-matched empirical background was constructed from all unique bin pairs in the extracted chromosome 1 region with the same bin separation, excluding the tested variant–promoter pair. The same-distance empirical background was constructed from all eligible bin pairs within the extracted chr1:108,750,000–109,800,000 region having the same bin separation as the tested contact. As a sensitivity analysis, promoter contacts were recalculated using only the single bin containing each transcription start site in the experimental 2-kb HepG2 Hi-C map and the 2,048-bp AlphaGenome FOLD_0 map. Each contact was expressed as its percentile among contacts spanning the same genomic distance.
A virtual-4C profile was extracted from the two-dimensional contact matrix by taking the rs12740374-containing anchor slice. For the smoothed profile, the three anchor bins and three target bins were averaged. The position of the profile maximum within ±10 kb of the SORT1 TSS was recorded. Because the profile was smoothed over an approximately 6-kb window, its offset from the annotated TSS was not interpreted at base-pair resolution.
Held-out AlphaGenome contact prediction
The rs12740374 interval is assigned to AlphaGenome data fold 0. To avoid evaluating the all-folds distilled model at a locus included in its training data, HepG2 contact maps were predicted with FOLD_0, for which fold 0 was excluded from training. Reference-allele contact predictions were averaged across the model's HepG2 contact-map tracks. Promoter contacts and same-distance backgrounds were calculated with the same 3 × 3-bin statistic used for the observed 2-kb map.
Because experimental and predicted contact maps undergo different preprocessing and are expressed on different scales, agreement between them was evaluated by promoter rank, same-distance percentile, and peak location rather than by equality of absolute values. This comparison is a held-out-locus evaluation against the experimental assay represented among AlphaGenome's training targets. It is not an independent biological replicate of that assay.
Allele-specific contact effects were evaluated as a control. Percentage change in implied observed/expected contact was calculated as 100 × [exp(ALT − REF) − 1]. Reference and alternate predictions were compared at the three promoters, and the SORT1 promoter change was referred to an empirical null of 100 single-nucleotide variants sampled within 10 kb of rs12740374. These allele-specific results are reported descriptively; the ALL_FOLDS empirical null was not recomputed under FOLD_0, and the two are not combined.


Cross-context AlphaGenome contact analysis
Reference-sequence contact maps were predicted for a 1,048,576-bp interval centered on rs12740374 using the standard AlphaGenome model, requesting all available CONTACT_MAPS outputs without an ontology filter. The resulting 28 tracks represented 12 biosample ontologies. Contact maps were provided at 2,048-bp resolution as distance-normalized log observed/expected values. Where multiple tracks belonged to the same ontology, their contact matrices were averaged before analysis. A variant-anchored contact profile was calculated as the mean of the row and column containing rs12740374, and promoter contact was summarized across the TSS-containing bin and its two adjacent bins. Each promoter value was converted to a midrank percentile relative to all contacts at the same genomic separation within the corresponding 1-Mb map.
Liver caQTL analysis
Liver caQTL summary statistics were obtained for 249 variants within hg38 chr1:109,250,000–109,400,000 that had been tested against the 28 accessibility peaks assigned to coordinated peak set 53927. The primary analysis used caQTL effect sizes at peak 15120, the accessibility peak containing rs12740374, and was restricted to the 80 variants with available allele-oriented European rs12740374 tagging coefficients. Experimental effects were harmonized to the AlphaGenome REF-to-ALT allele axis.
Each variant was scored independently using the ALL_FOLDS and FOLD_0 AlphaGenome training regimes with a 524,288-bp input centered on rs12740374. FOLD_0 excluded the rs12740374 locus from training. ATAC effects were calculated using the AlphaGenome center-mask variant scorer with DIFF_LOG2_SUM aggregation. Scores from ATAC outputs annotated as liver (UBERON:0002107) or HepG2 (EFO:0001187) were summed to obtain one liver-like REF-to-ALT accessibility score per variant. The same track-selection and aggregation procedure was applied to every variant under both training regimes. For each variant i, the rs12740374-tagging model was defined as 
M_i = D_i + t_i D_rs12740374, 
where D_i is the direct AlphaGenome ATAC effect and t_i = Cov(G_i, G_rs12740374) / Var(G_i) is the allele-oriented tagging coefficient estimated from European haplotypes. For rs12740374 itself, the tagging-model score was set equal to its direct effect to avoid double counting. Agreement with the observed caQTL effects was evaluated using Pearson and Spearman correlations across the 80 variants.
As sensitivity analyses, experimental caQTL effects were combined by inverse-variance weighting across the five focal peaks centered on driver peak 15120 (peaks 15118–15122) and across all 28 peaks in coordinated set 539, using 1 / Var(beta) as the weight. These represented alternative summaries of the experimental accessibility phenotype. The AlphaGenome values remained variant-level ATAC predictions and were not separate predictions for each experimental peak.
Reconstruction and prediction of Wang et al. CRISPR repair outcomes
Repair outcomes were extracted from the supplementary workbook of Wang et al.20. Source coordinates were reported on hg18 and mapped to GRCh38 using a locally verified offset of −344,145 bp. We validated this mapping by comparing the reported anchor allele of each repair outcome with the corresponding GRCh38 base. The anchor agreed for 379 of 380 outcomes, including all 24 human-hepatocyte products. The sole discordance was a low-frequency mouse-liver outcome anchored at rs12740374, for which the workbook reported the experimental minor T allele whereas GRCh38 contains the major G allele. This outcome was reconstructed on the intended rs12740374-T background; its deletion began one nucleotide downstream of rs12740374, and the deleted sequence matched that background exactly. It was not among the 50 outcomes retained for Figure 2B and did not enter the human-only analyses. Simple insertion and deletion outcomes were parsed from allele strings of the form */+[sequence] or */−[sequence]. Within each experiment, product frequency was calculated as the number of reads assigned to that product divided by the total number of reads assigned to reconstructable simple indels.
Each repair product was reconstructed on a 524,288-bp sequence carrying rs12740374-T. Insertions and deletions were applied at their mapped positions while maintaining a fixed model-input length by trimming or extending the distal downstream boundary. Within the human and locus-humanized mouse experiments separately, products were ranked by decreasing read count. Up to 50 products from each experiment were considered, identical reconstructed sequences were collapsed by sequence identity, and each sequence was assigned its better, i.e., lower numerical, within-experiment rank. The 50 unique sequences with the best ranks were retained, with ties resolved by the largest within-experiment frequency and then by allele label. Selection was independent of the AlphaGenome-predicted effect.
Each retained product was modeled as a homogeneous edited sequence. RNA output was summarized over ±2 kb around the transcription start sites of SORT1, PSRC1 and CELSR2. Primary analyses in Figure 2B and Figure S4 used liver RNA target index 4 in the stored AlphaGenome metadata: pooled child/adult liver poly(A)+ unstranded RNA-seq. Sensitivity analyses used the available gene- and strand-compatible postnatal tracks, i.e., target 0 for CELSR2 and target 2 for SORT1 and PSRC1, and repeated the top-50 outcome, deletion-grid, selected-junction, and human frequency-weighted analyses. For an edited sequence e and the unedited rs12740374-T sequence m, percentage RNA change was calculated as 100 × (RNAe − RNAm) / RNAm.
For the human-only frequency-weighted analysis, all 24 reconstructable simple products observed in primary human hepatocytes were included. These comprised 17 deletions and 7 insertions and represented 1,396 of 1,438 human indel reads, or 97.1%. For each gene, the contribution of product i was calculated by multiplying its predicted percentage RNA change by its frequency among the 1,396 modeled simple-indel reads. Contributions were summed across the 24 products to obtain a conditional frequency-weighted mean. This calculation conditions on a modeled indel having occurred and was not additionally multiplied by the bulk editing fraction. It approximates the repair mixture by averaging predictions generated separately for homogeneous sequences.
The systematic deletion scan used the inferred Cas9 cleavage boundary at chr1:109,274,966|109,274,967. All combinations of 0–10 bases removed upstream and 0–10 bases removed downstream were generated, yielding 121 geometries with total deletion lengths of 0–20 nt, including the unedited 0,0 condition. Each construct was scored independently on the rs12740374-T background and expressed as a percentage change relative to the unedited T sequence. For the repair-junction analysis, a selected descriptive subset of 30 products was drawn from the completed deletion grid. This subset comprised deletions of 1–10 upstream bases crossed with deletions of 0, 1, or 2 downstream bases. The reconstructed local sequence of every product was searched for the exact 10-bp sequence GTTGCTCAAT. Products with no downstream deletion retained the original sequence. Among products deleting one downstream base, three recreated the exact sequence across the repair junction and were classified as junction-restored, whereas the remaining seven were classified as disrupted. All ten products deleting two downstream bases were classified as disrupted.
Reanalysis of the Kircher et al. saturation-mutagenesis MPRA
Kircher et al. MPRA measurements were obtained for the 600-bp interval chr1:109,274,652–109,275,251. For the primary benchmark, measurements were averaged across the available forward-orientation SORT1 and SORT1.2 constructs for each substitution. Both measurements were available for 1,790 substitutions; eight additional substitutions were available in only one forward construct, yielding 1,798 substitutions with matched experimental and AlphaGenome measurements. The model-exposure sensitivity analysis was restricted to the 1,790 substitutions measured in both constructs.
All variants were evaluated on a construct background carrying rs12740374-T, such that the T>G substitution at rs12740374 restored the major allele. AlphaGenome variant scoring used a 16,384-bp interval for ATAC and H3K27ac and a 131,072-bp interval for RNA. Recommended AlphaGenome scorers were used for ATAC, histone ChIP-seq, and RNA-seq, with H3K27ac tracks selected using histone-mark metadata.
ATAC and H3K27ac effects were the mean signed score across matched HepG2 tracks. For RNA, compatible-strand HepG2 scores were averaged separately for SORT1, PSRC1, and CELSR2. Percentage changes were calculated only where explicitly stated. For sequence-prediction analyses, percentage changes were calculated from the perturbed and baseline RNA signals. In the Kircher benchmark, gene-level RNA variant-scorer log-fold-change values were converted as 100 × expm1(score). Because sequence-derived changes and variant-scorer outputs differ in their definitions and normalization, their absolute magnitudes were not compared across analyses. Pearson and Spearman correlations were calculated on complete cases. Ordinary least-squares lines were added for visualization only. Sensitivity analyses were repeated after excluding the three substitutions at the rs12740374 position.
For the model-exposure sensitivity analysis, Kircher effects were restricted to substitutions or single-base deletions measured in both forward-orientation constructs (SORT1 and SORT1.2), and the two Log2 MPRA effects were averaged within each edit. This produced one experimental value for each of 1,790 substitutions and 126 deletions. The orientation-reversed SORT1.flip construct was excluded. Identical edited sequences were evaluated with the default ALL_FOLDS model and with FOLD_0, whose training set excluded the genomic fold containing the SORT1 locus. Predictions were generated on the rs12740374-T background using the same sequence construction, HepG2 RNA, ATAC, and H3K27ac targets, and scoring procedures used for the primary ALL_FOLDS benchmark. RNA results were summarized for SORT1, PSRC1 and CELSR2 individually and as their arithmetic mean. Agreement with the construct-averaged MPRA effect was quantified using Pearson and Spearman correlations over the same matched edits for both model versions. 
Multi-element saturation-mutagenesis benchmark
Processed saturation-mutagenesis measurements and element metadata were obtained from the Kircher et al. study. Elements were required to have an unambiguous hg38 placement, reconciled reference and alternate alleles, quantitative single-substitution effects, a reported experimental cell line, and a matching AlphaGenome accessibility output. Six elements met these criteria: F9, FOXE1, LDLR, MYC rs11986220, PKLR, and SORT1. Only hg38 single-nucleotide variants represented by at least 10 barcodes were retained. LDLR and LDLR.2 measurements were averaged at matched substitutions, as were SORT1 and SORT1.2. The 48-hour PKLR assay was used as specified in the published element metadata. The harmonized comparison comprised 8,153 unique substitutions, including 1,784 for SORT1.
Each substitution was evaluated at its native genomic locus with a 16,384-bp prediction interval. The AlphaGenome response was the signed centre-masked DIFF_LOG2_SUM accessibility score. The frozen analysis plan specified experimental-cell-line-matched ATAC predictions for F9, LDLR, PKLR, and SORT1, with DNase as the prespecified fallback for FOXE1 and MYC rs11986220, for which matched ATAC outputs were unavailable. Because matched DNase outputs were available for all six cell lines, and because the original modality assignment was perfectly aligned with the element-level performance ordering, a post hoc harmonized analysis using DNase for every element was added and is reported as the common-modality comparison. ATAC estimates were retained as paired secondary measurements in the four contexts where they were available.
Within-element agreement with the published signed reporter effect was summarized separately for each assay by Spearman correlation with a 95% Fisher-transform confidence interval. Correlations were combined by assay-specific random-effects meta-analysis of Fisher-transformed coefficients, and heterogeneity was evaluated with Cochran's Q and I². The all-six DNase summary and the matched four-element DNase-versus-ATAC comparison were calculated separately, and paired assay estimates from the same element were never treated as independent elements within a single meta-analysis.
Figure 2E and Figure 2F use different scoring implementations and inclusion rules and are not directly comparable: Figure 2E reports Pearson and Spearman correlations from the recommended modality-specific variant scorers across 1,798 SORT1 substitutions, whereas Figure 2F reports Spearman correlations from centre-masked DIFF_LOG2_SUM accessibility scores under a common ≥10-barcode filter across elements, retaining 1,784 SORT1 substitutions. This benchmark compares episomal reporter effects with predicted native-locus accessibility and therefore tests shared sequence sensitivity rather than equivalence of assays or genomic contexts.
For each Kircher element outside SORT1, the identical substitution set and native-locus sequence constructs used in the primary ALL_FOLDS analysis were rescored with the AlphaGenome model that excluded the element's genomic fold from training. The matched models were FOLD_0 for F9, FOLD_1 for FOXE1 and MYC, FOLD_2 for PKLR, and FOLD_3 for LDLR. Predictions were summarized using the same cell-line-matched DNase tracks for all five elements and ATAC tracks for F9, LDLR and PKLR. ALL_FOLDS and held-out comparisons used identical variants, experimental MPRA effects, track selections and aggregation rules. Agreement was quantified within each element and modality using Spearman correlation. 95% confidence intervals were calculated after Fisher transformation.
Exhaustive regional single-nucleotide mutagenesis
Single-nucleotide mutagenesis was performed across hg38 chr1:109,224,968–109,324,968, a 100,001-bp interval centered on rs12740374. At every position, each of the three non-reference alleles was evaluated, yielding 300,003 sequence variants. Predictions were generated with the AlphaGenome ALL_FOLDS model using 524,288-bp input sequences centered on rs12740374. RNA-seq signal was averaged across AlphaGenome liver tracks annotated with UBERON:0002107 and summarized over ±2 kb around the GENCODE transcription start sites of SORT1, PSRC1 and CELSR2. For every variant and gene, the RNA effect was calculated as the prediction for the substituted sequence minus that for the unmodified reference sequence.
For each genomic position, we recorded the minimum, maximum, and maximum absolute RNA effect across the three alternative alleles. A coordinated RNA score was calculated for each variant as the smallest of the SORT1, PSRC1 and CELSR2 RNA changes when all three were positive, and as zero otherwise. The highest coordinated score among the three alternative alleles was retained at each position. This deliberately conservative score identifies substitutions predicted to increase all three genes.
Before the exhaustive scan, a preliminary two-stage screen was performed to nominate candidate regulatory windows. Consecutive 10-bp windows at 5-bp spacing were perturbed by nucleotide complementation (A:T and C:G) and ranked separately for each gene by the absolute difference between their liver RNA effect and their mean effect in subcutaneous and visceral adipose tissue. The 50 highest-ranked non-overlapping windows per gene were retained for the preliminary analysis. This preliminary screen was used only for annotation. The single-nucleotide traces and coordinated RNA score derive from the exhaustive 300,003-variant scan. 


Native-locus 501-bp substitution analysis
A 501-bp interval centered on rs12740374 was examined by single-nucleotide in silico mutagenesis in an otherwise native 524,288-bp sequence carrying the rs12740374-T allele. Each position from −250 to +250 bp was independently replaced by each of the three alternative nucleotides, producing 1,503 substituted sequences. Predictions were generated with AlphaGenome ALL_FOLDS. For each gene, RNA loss was calculated as the predicted liver RNA signal of the intact T-allele sequence minus that of the substituted T-background sequence, summarized over ±2 kb around the transcription start site. Figure 3B displays the equal-weight mean of the SORT1, PSRC1 and CELSR2 losses.
The H1–H6 windows were not selected from this three-gene mean. They were frozen from an earlier SORT1-specific nomination analysis. At each position, SORT1 sensitivity was defined as the largest positive RNA loss among the three possible substitutions. All 12-bp windows evaluated at 1-bp intervals were ranked by their mean positional sensitivity. After excluding windows overlapping the established C/EBP interval, the six highest-ranking windows were selected greedily with a 5-bp separation margin. The six highest-ranking windows were selected greedily with a 5-bp separation margin and labelled H1–H6 according to genomic position rather than effect rank. Their coordinates relative to rs12740374 were H1, −178 to −167 bp; H2, −152 to −141 bp; H3, −87 to −76 bp; H4, −61 to −50 bp; H5, −37 to −26 bp; and H6, +50 to +61 bp. These frozen windows were retained when the displayed endpoint was changed to the three-gene mean and were not reselected.
JASPAR motif-family compatibility analysis
The frozen H1–H6 windows and the C/EBP positive-control interval were compared with motifs from the JASPAR 2024 CORE vertebrate non-redundant collection. Motif matches were evaluated on both strands, and only native matches with a PWM-score fraction of at least 0.70 were retained. At each position, the substitution producing the largest equal-weight mean RNA loss across SORT1, PSRC1 and CELSR2 was used to construct the disrupted sequence for PWM rescoring. Thus, the window locations remained based on the frozen SORT1 nominations, whereas the substitutions used in the motif-disruption calculation were selected using the three-gene mean endpoint.
Motif-family compatibility was calculated from the native PWM match, the positive loss of PWM score after substitution, and the fraction of the motif overlapping the nominated window. These scores were used only to rank compatible motif families; they are not probabilities and do not demonstrate transcription-factor binding.
Held-out audit of native sequence-sensitive windows
After H1–H6 had been selected using ALL_FOLDS, a held-out audit was performed with FOLD_0, which excluded the SORT1 locus from training. For each window and the C/EBP positive-control interval, the nucleotide producing the greatest SORT1 loss at every position was installed simultaneously. The identical 524,288-bp T-background sequence was evaluated with ALL_FOLDS and FOLD_0.
The prespecified primary endpoint was agreement in the direction of the SORT1 loss. Secondary endpoints were the Spearman correlation of the seven SORT1 window scores and direction agreement across all 21 gene-window combinations for SORT1, PSRC1 and CELSR2. This selected-window audit assesses whether the local sensitivity pattern survives locus holdout; it is not an unbiased genome-wide benchmark and does not validate transcription-factor binding. 


Native-locus directional scramble-recovery analysis
All scramble-retention analyses used 524,288-bp inputs and eight prespecified root seeds: 1,740,374–1,740,381. Composition-preserving shuffles were generated using numpy.random.default_rng. Deterministic arm- and component-specific random streams were derived from each root seed, and identical shuffled templates were used in matched rs12740374-G and -T constructs. Within each seed, identical scramble templates were used for the G and T sequences so that the two constructs differed only at rs12740374. Sequence was shuffled mononucleotide-wise, preserving nucleotide composition. RNA signal was averaged across AlphaGenome liver tracks annotated with UBERON:0002107 and summarized over ±2 kb around each gene transcription start site.


For a perturbed construct, retention was defined as:
retention = |T − G RNA effect in the perturbed construct| / |T − G RNA effect at the intact locus|.
To measure directional recovery, the native C/EBP interval from −1 to +8 bp was protected while the non-motif sequence from −1 kb to −2 bp and from +9 bp to +1 kb was independently scrambled. Native sequence was then progressively restored on one side while the opposite arm remained scrambled. Upstream-recovery constructs restored sequence progressively toward −1 kb, whereas downstream-recovery constructs restored sequence progressively toward +1 kb. Means and standard errors were calculated across the eight seeds. This analysis measures recovery within a scrambled native-locus background and does not establish autonomous enhancer sufficiency.


Two-dimensional boundary scan
Joint upstream and downstream boundaries were evaluated directly rather than inferred by adding the two directional recovery curves. For an upstream extent U and downstream extent D, the candidate interval comprised the native sequence from −U through +D relative to rs12740374, inclusive. Two matched perturbations were generated for every boundary pair:
* In the outside-scramble condition, the candidate interval was retained while its upstream and downstream flanks within the local ±1-kb region were independently scrambled.
* In the inside-scramble condition, the candidate interval was scrambled while the surrounding sequence remained native; rs12740374 was held fixed.
Every geometry was evaluated on matched G and T backgrounds across the eight seeds. All outside-scramble T constructs contained the complete minor-allele C/EBP sequence from −1 through +8 bp.
The initial frozen candidate set comprised 1,150 boundary pairs. It combined a broad 5-bp guard grid with a one-base-resolution surface spanning upstream extents of 176–190 bp and downstream extents of 120–160 bp. A candidate passed the mean criterion when:
1. mean SORT1 outside-scramble retention was at least 0.70;
2. mean SORT1 inside-scramble retention was no more than 0.30; and
3. the complete minor-allele C/EBP sequence was retained.
The median-based robustness criterion replaced the first condition with median outside-scramble retention of at least 0.70 while retaining the same inside-scramble threshold. Candidates were ranked first by inclusive length, then by higher outside-scramble mean retention, and finally by smaller downstream extent. The frozen scan identified −179/+134, or 314 bp inclusive, as the shortest mean-passing candidate and −179/+135, or 315 bp inclusive, as the shortest median-passing candidate.
For the wider heatmap in Figure 3F, the local one-base-resolution surface was subsequently completed across upstream extents of 177–190 bp and downstream extents of 105–160 bp. This added 260 previously unscored boundary pairs. The completed surface identified −179/+107, or 287 bp inclusive, as the shortest mean-passing candidate. Its median outside-scramble retention remained below 0.70, whereas the 315-bp interval remained the shortest median-passing candidate. A separate post hoc sensitivity analysis completed the 44 × 64 Cartesian product of the upstream and downstream coordinates represented in the broader search. These extensions assess the stability of the boundary result and were not used to redefine the prespecified operational interval.
Accordingly, the interval from −179 to +135 bp, corresponding to hg38 chr1:109,274,789–109,275,103, was retained as the 315-bp operational construct. The selected boundaries are model-derived limits under the specified scramble design and should not be interpreted as nucleotide-resolution biochemical enhancer boundaries.
Component-necessity analysis within the 315-bp interval
The contribution of individual sequence components was evaluated within the fixed 315-bp interval from −179 to +135 bp. Composition-preserving scrambles were generated separately for H1–H6, the C/EBP interval, the upstream arm from −179 to −2, the downstream arm from +9 to +135, and both non-motif arms. An additional full-interval control scrambled the complete 315-bp interval while holding rs12740374 fixed. The same scramble was used in the matched G and T constructs for each of the eight seeds.
Retention was calculated relative to the intact-locus G-to-T RNA effect separately for SORT1, PSRC1, and CELSR2. The arithmetic mean of the three gene-specific retention values under ALL_FOLDS is displayed in Figure 3G. Gene-resolved ALL_FOLDS results and the corresponding FOLD_0 results are reported in Figure S8E. This is a necessity analysis within an already selected interval; it does not independently redefine its boundaries or identify the transcription factors responsible for the predicted effects.


Promoter-proximal transfer of the 315-bp interval
The transferred sequence comprised the 315-bp interval extending 179 bp upstream through 135 bp downstream of rs12740374 (GRCh38 chr1:109,274,789–109,275,103). Three donor sequences were evaluated: the rs12740374-T sequence, the otherwise identical rs12740374-G sequence and a composition-preserving scrambled T sequence in which rs12740374 was retained. Donors were installed by strand-aware, length-preserving replacement of the native recipient sequence.
Recipient genes were ranked using liver consensus normalized transcripts per million (nTPM) from Human Protein Atlas version 24.1 and divided into the 500 lowest-, middle-, and highest-expressing cohorts. A frozen subset of 100 low-expression genes was evaluated at replacement distances of 0, 20, 30, 100, 1,000, 10,000, 100,000 and 500,000 bp upstream of the TSS. All three 500-gene cohorts were evaluated at 30 bp. Twenty-nine genes were excluded because a 1,048,576-bp input interval containing the recipient TSS and replacement site could not be accommodated within chromosome boundaries, leaving 498, 486, and 487 genes in the low-, middle-, and high-expression cohorts, respectively.
Predictions used AlphaGenome ALL_FOLDS with 1,048,576-bp inputs. Liver RNA was summarized over ±2 kb around each recipient TSS using gene-strand-compatible UBERON:0002107 tracks. Absolute effects were calculated as construct RNA minus native-recipient RNA. Relative effects were calculated as log₂(construct RNA/native RNA), and the allele-dependent effect was calculated as T minus G. Human Protein Atlas and native AlphaGenome expression were compared after log₁₀ transformations of nTPM + 0.1 and predicted RNA + 0.0001, respectively.
Distal contact-matched replacement analysis
Chromosome 1 promoters were defined as ±5 kb around one GENCODE release 46 TSS per gene. Experimental contacts were obtained from the GRCh38 HepG2 in situ Hi-C map 4DNFICSTCJQZ and represented as KR-balanced observed/expected contact at 5-kb resolution. Candidate replacement sites were restricted to 50 kb–1 Mb from the promoter and excluded annotated promoter, exon and gene-body regions.
For each eligible promoter, a high-contact site was selected from three-bin neighborhoods in the promoter-specific upper contact decile, with mean observed/expected contact of at least 1. Two low-contact controls were selected from the local bottom contact quartile. Controls were required to lie on the same side of the promoter and within 75 kb of the high-contact site, have a GC-content mismatch of no more than 0.08, and be separated from the other selected sites by at least 15 kb.
The same forward-oriented 315-bp T- and G-containing sequences were introduced at the high-contact and two low-contact sites by length-preserving replacement. All seven states were scored within the same promoter-specific 1,048,576-bp interval, positioned to contain the recipient TSS and all three replacement sites.
For promoter g, the contact-associated interaction was calculated as:
Interactiong = (T − G)high − mean[(T − G)low1, (T − G)low2].
A positive value indicates that the experimentally high-contact site produced the larger allele-dependent RNA response. Contact contrast was defined as the high-site observed/expected value minus the mean observed/expected value of the two controls. Promoters were summarized in the exploratory distance bands 50–150, 150–300, 300–500, 500–750 and 750–1,000 kb. Confidence intervals were obtained from 5,000 bootstrap resamples of observed contact-edge clusters using seed 12,740,374. For the contact dose–response, promoters were divided into within-distance contact-contrast quintiles and plotted at the actual median contact contrast of each group.
Because a single promoter-specific input interval had to contain the recipient TSS and all three replacement sites, progressively less flanking sequence context was available around one or more endpoints at larger genomic separations. Analyses beyond 500 kb are therefore subject to input-boundary geometry and were not interpreted as establishing a biological distance limit.
Tissue-specific rs12740374 RNA effects
The rs12740374 G>T substitution was scored using the recommended AlphaGenome RNA-seq variant scorer on 1,048,576-bp inputs. Gene-strand-compatible tracks were selected for liver, adipose, spleen, lung, brain, heart and kidney. Subcutaneous and visceral adipose tracks were averaged to obtain the adipose value; tracks within the other ontologies were averaged after gene-strand filtering. The same sequences, ontology definitions and aggregation procedure were applied to ALL_FOLDS and FOLD_0. Concordance was assessed across the 21 tissue–gene combinations using Pearson correlation, Spearman correlation and sign agreement.
Context-specific regional mutagenesis
All three alternative substitutions were evaluated at each position within ±50 kb of rs12740374. RNA effects were summarized for SORT1, PSRC1 and CELSR2 using liver, CD8+ memory T-cell and CD14+ monocyte tracks. For each substitution, the coordinated RNA score was defined as the smallest of the three gene effects when all were positive and as zero otherwise. The highest coordinated score among the three alternative alleles was displayed at each position.
Cell-type-associated motif-replacement screen
Eight motifs representing liver-associated factors, HNF1B (MA0153.2), HNF4A (MA0114.5), FOXA2 (MA0047.4), and CEBPA (MA0102.5), and immune-cell-associated factors, SPI1 (MA0080.7), RUNX1 (MA0002.3), IRF8 (MA0652.2), and RELA (MA0107.1), were obtained from the JASPAR 2024 CORE vertebrate collection. Each motif was installed at every position across the 100,001-bp interval centered on rs12740374 by length-preserving replacement of an equal-length segment of native sequence. Each replacement was evaluated in both forward and reverse-complement orientations using 524,288-bp inputs and the AlphaGenome ALL_FOLDS model.
For each replacement, predicted RNA changes relative to the unmodified native sequence were summed across SORT1, PSRC1 and CELSR2. No rs12740374-only effect was subtracted. At each position, the orientation producing the larger signed three-gene sum was retained. Thus, the displayed value remained negative when both orientations were predicted to be repressive. Values were clipped symmetrically at the 99.75th percentile for visualization.
Because each perturbation simultaneously installed a motif and removed an equal-length native segment, the screen cannot distinguish motif activity from disruption of the replaced sequence. It was therefore treated as an exploratory map of motif-dependent sequence receptivity rather than evidence of transcription-factor occupancy or a calibrated positional atlas.
Matched-control and held-out audit of motif-replacement candidates
Before inspecting the matched-control or FOLD_0 results, five primary candidates were frozen within each cellular context. Candidates were selected as the highest signed net-output sites among the prespecified context-associated motifs, with a minimum separation of 500 bp between selected sites. This produced 15 candidate–context combinations corresponding to 14 unique sequence constructs.
For every candidate, 199 unique mononucleotide-composition-preserving shuffles of the corresponding motif were generated using recorded seeds. Each shuffled sequence replaced the identical native interval and therefore matched the candidate in length, nucleotide composition, genomic position, and removed reference sequence. Both orientations of every shuffled sequence were evaluated, and the orientation with the larger signed three-gene effect was retained using the same rule applied to the motif.
The control-adjusted effect was defined as the candidate’s signed summed RNA effect minus the median effect of its 199 matched shuffles. A one-sided empirical P value was calculated as:
P = (1 + number of matched shuffles with an effect at least as large as the candidate effect) / 200.
Empirical P values were adjusted across the 15 primary candidates using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure. A candidate passed the matched-control criterion when its control-adjusted effect was positive and its adjusted q value was below 0.10. The prespecified ALL_FOLDS gate required at least eight passing candidates overall and at least one in each cellular context.
The frozen candidate constructs and matched controls were subsequently rescored using FOLD_0, which excluded the genomic fold containing the SORT1 locus from training. The prespecified FOLD_0 gate required at least 10 of 15 candidates to retain positive control-adjusted effects, a Spearman correlation of at least 0.50 between ALL_FOLDS and FOLD_0 control-adjusted effects, and a positive empirical effect for the top candidate in each context. Coordinated activation was assigned only when the gene-specific effects on SORT1, PSRC1, and CELSR2 were all positive.
Statistical analysis and visualization
All analyses were computational; no new biological replicates were generated. Summary statistics are reported as means or medians as indicated. Shaded bands in the scramble-retention analysis represent the standard error of the mean across eight independently shuffled sequences.
Pearson and Spearman correlations were calculated using pairwise-complete observations, and the statistic used is specified for each analysis; Pearson's r and Spearman's ρ are distinguished throughout and are not interchangeable. Confidence intervals for individual Spearman correlations used the Fisher transformation, and the multi-element summary used random-effects meta-analysis on the Fisher-z scale with heterogeneity assessed by Cochran's Q and I². Bootstrap intervals used the resampling unit and random seed prespecified for the corresponding analysis.
Dense mutagenesis and motif-replacement screens were treated as exploratory and were not adjusted for multiple testing. The 15 frozen motif candidates were evaluated using one-sided empirical P values from 199 matched shuffles per candidate, followed by Benjamini–Hochberg correction. Prespecified thresholds were reported regardless of outcome; the motif rank-stability threshold of Spearman’s ρ ≥ 0.50 was not met.
Analyses and figures were generated in Python using NumPy, pandas, SciPy, scikit-learn, and Matplotlib.
